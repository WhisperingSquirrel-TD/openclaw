What this looks like in practice

It reads your files

Point it at a folder of receipts, meeting notes, and a draft budget. Ask for an expense report. It pulls data from the right sources and structures the output.

It cross-references

Give it a new council policy and your existing safeguarding document. It reads both, compares them, and flags what’s changed and what you need to act on.

It works while you don’t

Assign a task from your phone. Come back to find the work done on your desktop. Not quite an employee — but not just a chatbot anymore either.

In practice

Four things charities
are doing right now

01 — The biggest time sink

Grant applications
& funding bids

Point AI at your funder guidelines, last year’s successful application, and your project notes. It produces a complete first draft of the application pack — structured to the funder’s requirements.
Some funders have said they can spot AI-generated applications. The human edit isn’t optional — it’s what makes the bid yours.
02 — The quarterly scramble

Impact reporting
& board papers

AI reads across your meeting notes, financial data, and programme outcomes. Instead of copying between systems for days, you get a structured board-ready brief to review and refine.
AI will confidently present a number it’s averaged incorrectly. The human review is where the value gets created.
03 — One person writes everything

Stakeholder
communications

Set permanent instructions about your charity’s voice and audience. AI drafts your newsletter from last month’s activity, in your tone, ready for review. Not generic output — output that sounds like you.
AI doesn’t know your relationships. It doesn’t know what you said to a funder last week. Always read before sending.
04 — The 80-page inbox bomb

Policy &
compliance

AI reads a new council policy document, cross-references your existing policies, and flags what’s changed and what you need to act on. Some tools do this directly from the web page — no download needed.
Public documents are fine. Internal documents with personal data — be cautious about which tool you use and where that data goes.
The question underneath

AI is not
automation

Automation is predictable — same input, same output.
AI is a black box that deals in probability, not certainty.
Ask it the same question twice — you’ll get different answers.

The question isn’t “do I trust AI?”
It’s “how do I build systems that make it trustworthy?”

Building trust in something fuzzy

Layer 1: Start low, build up

Begin with tasks where a mistake doesn’t matter. Use it ten times. Compare output to what you’d have produced. Calibrate. Then move up the sensitivity ladder with your eyes open.

Layer 2: Human-in-the-loop by design

AI drafts. A named human reviews. Output goes out under that human’s name. Not a safety net bolted on later — the way the work gets done from day one.

Layer 3: Name the boundary

One page: “We use AI for X. We don’t use it for Y. A named person reviews everything.” That clarity protects your staff, your beneficiaries, and your reputation.

Looking forward

This is the slowest
AI will ever be

The next twelve months

AI that knows you

Your policies, your templates, your funder requirements, your tone of voice. Set it up once — every interaction after that is contextual.

Content you can’t distinguish

Text, images, audio, video. Charities will need to decide how they use AI-generated content and how they disclose it.

Cost falls to near zero

What costs a subscription today will be free next year. The financial barrier is effectively gone. Only time, confidence, and governance remain.

Regulation arrives

The Charity Governance Code now explicitly references AI. Trustees who haven’t discussed it aren’t being cautious — they’re falling short.
